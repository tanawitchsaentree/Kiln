# Technique — type violence

Loaded when T sits at 7 or above. Concrete moves for spending the type axis, pulled from print and
signage traditions that had to make type carry hierarchy without colour or without room for a
second typeface.

## Contrast of era, not just of weight

Pairing a display face from one type era against a body face from another — a Victorian wood-type
display against a plain grotesque body, or a humanist serif display against a mono body — produces
contrast a weight change alone cannot. The two faces have to share nothing except legibility at
their respective sizes. This is riskier than weight contrast and reads far less like a template.

## Extreme scale jump, not a smooth ratio

A quiet system's scale ratio is smooth and predictable, per `references/foundations/grid.md`'s
sibling concerns. A loud type system can break the ratio once, deliberately, for one role — a
single display size several multiples larger than the next step down, used exactly once per screen.
The break has to be rare to keep meaning; used on every heading it stops being a break and becomes
the new ratio.

## Type doing the job colour usually does

Letterspacing, case, and weight can carry a status distinction that a quiet system would reach for
colour to make. All-caps with wide tracking for one category, lowercase with tight tracking for
another — legible from the type alone under forced-colours mode, which a colour-only distinction
never survives.

## Numerals as a design decision, not a default

Tabular versus proportional, oldstyle versus lining — pick deliberately and state it, per
`references/foundations/dataviz.md`'s tabular-figures rule, which applies to any numeral-heavy
interface, not only charts. A loud type system often has room for oldstyle figures in running text
and must still switch to tabular the moment numbers align in a column.

## Optical margin alignment

Punctuation and certain letterforms (quotation marks, hyphens, capital T and W) sit slightly outside
the geometric margin so the text block reads as aligned rather than merely being aligned. Print
typesetting does this by hand; on the web it needs an explicit negative margin on the offending
character, and it is one of the details that separates typeset from merely-set type.

## Where this axis breaks

Extreme type violence at small interface sizes breaks first at body-copy density — a display face
chosen for its drama rarely has a well-hinted small-size cut, and forcing it there produces text
that is technically legible and genuinely unpleasant to read for more than a sentence. Reserve the
loud face for display roles and let a quiet face carry body copy even in a T9 system; the contrast
between them is itself part of the loud axis's payoff.
